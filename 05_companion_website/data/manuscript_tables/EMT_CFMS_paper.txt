ABSTRACT


Barrett's esophagus progresses through epithelial plasticity toward adenocarcinoma, yet the physical organization of proteins underlying transitional states remains poorly understood. Here, patient-matched proteomics identified epithelial–mesenchymal plasticity across Barrett's progression, motivating time-resolved native complexome mapping in dynamic epithelial models. Hybrid states exhibited distinct chimeric assembly architectures and selective functional vulnerabilities not apparent from protein abundance alone. Projection of these experimentally resolved assembly programs into patient proteomes revealed persistent hybrid-program enrichment across the metaplasia–dysplasia–carcinoma trajectory, recurrence across epithelial cancers, and convergence on endolysosomal trafficking machinery. Exploratory spatial imaging detected hybrid-state protein proximity signals in human dysplastic lesions, revealing trends toward elevated complex abundance in progressors and defining an immune-excluded microenvironment. Targeted perturbation of assembly-nominated regulators altered T-cell effector profiles, providing functional evidence that this machinery can influence epithelial–immune communication. Together, these findings establish protein assembly dynamics as an actionable physical layer of cell-state plasticity, linking endolysosomal function to multiscale tissue organization and progression-associated immune remodeling.


Running title: Hybrid assemblies link Barrett’s plasticity to immune remodeling

INTRODUCTION
Barrett’s esophagus (BE) provides a clinically staged model of neoplastic progression in which metaplastic epithelium can advance through high-grade dysplasia (HGD) to esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) (Giroux and Rustgi 2017). Barrett’s progression has been extensively characterized at the genomic, transcriptional and stromal levels (Maslyonkina et al. 2021). Recurrent somatic alterations track with histological stage (Weaver et al. 2014), single-cell and spatial studies reveal early acquisition of cancer-associated immunosuppressive stromal features (Strasser et al. 2025), and cell-marker panels can stratify progression risk within non-dysplastic BE (Critchley-Thorne et al. 2016). Yet the cellular programs that connect these transitions and tissue states remain incompletely understood.
Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a conserved program that enables cellular plasticity during neoplastic progression (Lambert and Weinberg 2021), rather than simply a binary conversion between epithelial and mesenchymal identities. Cells can occupy transient hybrid states that retain epithelial cohesion while acquiring motility, stress tolerance and phenotypic flexibility, thereby supporting tissue invasion, dissemination and therapy resistance (Yang et al. 2020; Marjanovic et al. 2020; Jolly et al. 2016). Gene regulatory networks explain how these states arise and are maintained (Migault et al. 2022), while single-cell and spatial approaches reveal where they occur (Chang et al. 2025). Neither, however, determines how the underlying molecular components are physically organized into the macromolecular machinery that executes adhesion, trafficking, metabolism, cytoskeletal remodeling and stress adaptation.
Hybrid states therefore pose a fundamental biochemical question: how do cells physically organize the machinery required to execute epithelial and mesenchymal functions simultaneously? Because proteins act through dynamic multisubunit assemblies, plasticity may involve not only altered protein abundance but also redistribution of individual components into distinct functional machinery. Such organization is largely invisible to transcriptomic and conventional proteomic measurements and is particularly difficult to resolve in sample-limited human specimens. Whether hybrid states possess a corresponding physical assembly architecture, and whether that architecture contributes to the evolving tissue microenvironment during BE progression, remain unknown.
Native complexome profiling directly resolves such molecular organization (Goel et al. 2024; Havugimana et al. 2022), but its substantial sample-input requirements constrain direct analysis of routine endoscopic biopsies (McWhite et al. 2021) and has not been applied across Barrett’s progression. We therefore mapped time-resolved native protein assemblies across dynamic EMT and Barrett’s-derived epithelial models and projected the experimentally resolved programs into patient proteomes. This strategy allowed us to ask whether hybrid plasticity has a distinct physical architecture that is invisible to protein abundance, whether that architecture recurs during human Barrett’s progression, and whether its components influence the surrounding tissue environment.
We identify discrete epithelial, intermediate-hybrid and mesenchymal assembly programs and show that the hybrid state is distinguished by a transient chimeric assembly architecture, rather than simply combining endpoint features. These programs recur in patient tissue and across epithelial cancers, with hybrid organization in Barrett’s progression converging on endolysosomal trafficking machinery. Spatial analysis further links complexome-nominated endolysosomal protein proximity signals to local immune tissue organization, while targeted perturbation of assembly-nominated regulators alters T-cell effector profiles. Thus, dynamic protein assemblies provide an actionable physical layer of cellular plasticity connecting molecular organization to tissue architecture and lesion–immune communication during neoplastic progression.
RESULTS
Hybrid epithelial–mesenchymal plasticity persists across Barrett’s progression
Barrett’s esophagus can progress through high-grade dysplasia (HGD) to esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC), yet the protein programs accompanying this trajectory remain incompletely defined. We therefore performed deep quantitative proteomics on 54 biopsies from 27 patients spanning BE, HGD and EAC, including patient-matched histologically normal adjacent tissue (NAT) (Figure 1A; Figure S1A; Table S1). After quality control, 6,909 proteins were quantified, and principal-component analysis separated lesions from matched NAT across progression (Figure 1B; Figure S1B,C).
Patient-matched lesion–NAT comparisons identified 1,405 differentially abundant proteins in BE, 817 in HGD and 629 in EAC (FDR < 0.05, |log2 fold-change| ≥ 0.6; Figure 1C). Among 1,797 proteins altered in at least one stage, 322 were consistently altered across all three stages, defining both shared and stage-specific proteomic changes during progression (Figure 1D; Figure S1D; Table S1). Pathway analysis identified 23 significantly altered Hallmark programs, with angiogenesis and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) among the most prominently increased in lesions (Figure 1E). Complementary Gene Ontology and Reactome enrichment revealed shared and stage-specific pathway remodeling across BE, HGD and EAC (Figure S1E; Table S2).
Given the prominence and persistence of this signal across disease stages, we next resolved its underlying epithelial and mesenchymal features using an established pan-cancer EMT signature (Mak et al. 2016). Twenty-nine measured components (11 predominantly epithelial and 18 mesenchymal proteins) were significantly altered in at least one disease stage (Figure 1F). Patient-matched analyses revealed increased mesenchymal enrichment while substantial epithelial features were retained across lesion stages (Figure 1F,G). Thus, Barrett’s lesions retain epithelial protein programs alongside increased mesenchymal enrichment across the metaplasia–dysplasia–carcinoma trajectory, revealing persistent epithelial–mesenchymal program coexistence during progression.
These findings raised a mechanistic question not accessible from abundance measurements: does hybrid plasticity correspond to a distinct physical organization of the proteome? We therefore applied time-resolved native complexome profiling to determine how protein assemblies reorganize across epithelial, hybrid and mesenchymal states.
Native complexome mapping reveals conserved assembly architecture during epithelial plasticity
To resolve the physical organization underlying epithelial plasticity, we performed extensive biochemical fractionation of co-complexed proteins in soluble lysates from complementary epithelial models (Figure 2A). Barrett’s-derived esophageal (CP-A) cells provided a disease-relevant model of non-dysplastic specialized metaplasia (A.Palanca-Wessels et al. 2003), while the well-characterized TGFβ-responsive MCF10A EMT model system (Paul et al. 2023) enabled finer temporal resolution of dynamic epithelial–mesenchymal transition. Together, these models allowed us to test whether assembly remodeling observed during a temporally controlled transition is conserved in Barrett’s-derived epithelium.
Native protein assemblies were resolved by stacked high-performance ion-exchange chromatography and quantified by multiplexed (Tandem Mass Tag, TMT) mass spectrometry across biological replicates (Figure 2A; Figure S2A,B). Machine-learning EPIC analysis (Hu et al. 2019) of the chromatographic co-elution profiles resolved 38,591 co-complex associations among 4,315 proteins in CP-A cells and 9,503 high-confidence associations among 1,961 proteins across the MCF10A time course (Figure 2A; Table S3). EPIC’s interaction classifier achieved an AUC of 0.941 against curated positive and negative reference sets, while only 30 inferred associations overlapped stringent Negatome non-interactions (Figure S2D-F). 
We next asked whether assembly architecture was conserved across the two epithelial lineages. Dynamic time warping aligned their mesenchymal-program trajectories at corresponding TGFβ exposure times (Figure 2B). Among 7,348 MCF10A associations whose components were represented in CP-A, almost half (3,477, 47.3%) were independently recovered in Barrett’s-derived cells (Figure 2C). Recovery increased with MCF10A interaction confidence and was enriched 8.35-fold over degree-preserving randomized networks (empirical P = 0.0164; Figure 2D,E). Moreover, 3,086 of the shared associations (88.8%) were stably detected across CP-A profiling, including focal-adhesion, Retromer and proteasome machinery (Table S3). Thus, substantial native assembly architecture was conserved between the dynamic MCF10A reference and Barrett’s-derived epithelium.
Partitioning the interaction network resolved 611 heteromeric protein assemblies, encompassing both conserved and MCF10A-enriched machinery across major cellular compartments (Figure 2F–I; Table S4). The assemblies recovered well-established complexes, including the IDH3 (mitochondrial TCA enzyme complex), B-WICH (chromatin remodeling), AP1 adaptor (clathrin vesicle trafficking) complexes, providing an internal benchmark for the fidelity of complex reconstruction. Comparison against CORUM identified 158 assemblies with close reference matches, whereas 453 (74.1%) lacked a closely matching annotated complex, defined by a maximum Jaccard similarity below 0.3 across CORUM references. Jaccard similarity measures the number of shared protein members divided by the total number of distinct members in the inferred and reference assemblies (Figure 2G,H). These assemblies extend the epithelial assembly map beyond closely matching CORUM annotations, in addition to nominating alternative configurations of known machinery and candidate protein assemblies. The study is accompanied by a web portal (https://emili-laboratory.shinyapps.io/emt_cfms/; Figure S7).
Temporal assembly remodeling resolves state-specific plasticity programs
We next asked whether epithelial plasticity is accompanied by state-specific reorganization of native protein assemblies. Temporal complexome trajectories resolved 235 multisubunit assemblies into three programs (Figure 3A; Table S6): 82 epithelial-legacy (EL) assemblies declined across the transition, 62 intermediate-hybrid (IH) assemblies peaked transiently at day 4, and 91 mesenchymal-emerging (ME) assemblies accumulated through day 8. Thus, the intermediate state was not simply transitional between epithelial and mesenchymal endpoints but exhibited a distinct, temporally restricted program of protein assembly organization. Analysis of individual pair-wise interactions further revealed dynamic rewiring within these programs, particularly among the subunits of complexes involved in adhesion, extracellular-matrix organization, signaling, trafficking and metabolism (Figure S4; Table S7).
To determine whether dynamic complexome associations could be resolved to candidate physical interfaces, we generated structural models for 8,937 protein pairs using an ensemble of PyTorchAF-2024 and AlphaFold-Multimer predictions (Figure 3B). Structural confidence was benchmarked against randomized protein pairs and experimentally resolved structures, supporting discrimination of candidate physical interfaces (Figure S3A–D). Among 7,725 scored pairs, 537 satisfied stringent criteria for interface prioritization, including 232 without corresponding structures in the PDB reference set used for benchmarking (Figure 3C; Table S8). Integration with temporal assembly trajectories revealed structurally supported interactions across all three state programs (Figure 3D). Representative interfaces involving PTK2–CTTN, RAB7A–TBC1D5 and VPS35-containing Retromer machinery highlighted the structural organization of adhesion and endomembrane trafficking systems implicated in epithelial plasticity (Figure 3E).
Integration of matched temporal phosphoproteomics data (Paul et al. 2023) nominated 138 phosphosite-containing interface hypotheses across 93 unique phosphopeptides (Figure 3F; Table S9). The BRCA1–AFAP1Ser668 and MDC1–CTNND1Thr869 models nominate phosphorylation-dependent connections between actin and junctional regulators (Qian et al. 2002) and DNA-damage-response proteins with established phosphoprotein-recognition functions (Yu et al. 2003; Stucki et al. 2005). The YWHAH–PDCD4Ser457 model connects a 14-3-3-family protein to a translational regulator whose phosphorylation promotes association with 14-3-3 proteins (Galan et al. 2014). Together, these models nominate phosphoregulated interfaces linking cytoskeletal and junctional organization, translational control and DNA-damage responses during epithelial plasticity.
Together, these analyses establish coordinated temporal reorganization of native protein assemblies during epithelial plasticity and identify a structurally supported intermediate-hybrid program containing candidate phosphoregulatory interfaces. We therefore next asked whether this transient state program possesses an assembly architecture distinct from either endpoint.
Chimeric assemblies define the intermediate-hybrid state and expose functional vulnerabilities
The temporal assembly programs raised a fundamental question: does the intermediate-hybrid state represent a partial mixture of epithelial and mesenchymal features, or a distinct physical organization of the proteome? Intermediate-hybrid assemblies formed chimeric networks that physically connected cellular systems otherwise associated with distinct state functions (Figure 4A; Table S6). These networks linked epithelial-junctional machinery (CDH1, CTNNA1, CTNNB1, JUP) with focal-adhesion and actin regulators (TLN1, VCL, PTK2, ACTN1), while interconnected modules incorporated endosomal trafficking, metabolic stress, RNA processing and DNA-damage machinery. Thus, rather than simply retaining components of both endpoints, the hybrid state reorganized them into a distinct assembly architecture integrating epithelial organization with migratory and stress-adaptive programs.
We experimentally tested representative interactions spanning these chimeric modules and functional systems. Endogenous co-immunoprecipitation recovered CTNND1–MDC1, PABPC1–CSDE1, QRICH1–FUBP3, HNRNPR–ILF3, CSDE1–RCC1 and PDCD4–14-3-3 pair-wise interactions (Figure 4B), while TBC1D5–RAB7A co-association persisted across the TGFβ time course (Figure 4C). These orthogonal measurements supported co-complex associations spanning junctional organization, RNA regulation and endosomal trafficking.
We next investigated whether the remodeled assembly architecture was associated with state-selective functional vulnerabilities. Integration with a previous anti-motility screen (Paul et al. 2023) highlighted pharmacologically tractable targets associated with dynamically remodeled network modules, particularly those involving cytoskeletal organization and adhesion-linked signaling (Figure 4A,E). To test whether these vulnerabilities were selective for the EMT-associated state rather than motility more generally, we examined Hesperadin, an Aurora kinase inhibitor identified in the screen, under TGFβ- (EMT) versus EGF-induced (control) motility. Hesperadin strongly impaired TGFβ-induced motility (t = 6.81, adjusted P < 0.0001), but had a modest, non-significant effect on EGF-induced motility (t = –2.14, adjusted P = 0.065; Figure 4E). Hesperadin preferentially inhibited motility in the TGFβ-induced EMT state compared with EGF stimulation (F = 81.1, P < 2×10-16).
Together, these findings identify chimeric protein assemblies as a physical feature of intermediate-hybrid plasticity and link remodeled assembly networks to pharmacologically modifiable EMT-associated motility. We next asked whether these experimentally resolved hybrid assembly programs recur during clinical Barrett’s progression.
Hybrid assemblies recur across epithelial cancers and converge on endolysosomal trafficking
We next asked whether the experimentally resolved assembly programs were engaged in human disease. Stage-stratified analysis of Barrett’s proteomes identified 45 IH or ME assemblies enriched in lesions relative to normal adjacent tissue across one or more stages of the metaplasia–dysplasia–carcinoma trajectory (Figure 5A). Barrett’s lesions preferentially engaged intermediate-hybrid rather than end-stage mesenchymal programs. Hybrid Balance Scores integrating IH assembly enrichment with epithelial–mesenchymal program balance were increased in lesions relative to matched NAT (P = 0.0021, FDR q = 0.0099; Figure 5B), while the IH component was independently enriched (P = 0.0002, FDR q = 0.0016; Figure 5C). Thus, the experimentally defined intermediate-hybrid program is retained across human Barrett’s progression.
We then asked whether these Barrett’s-associated assembly programs generalized beyond this disease. Testing the fixed set of 45 assemblies across five independent CPTAC epithelial cancer cohorts revealed extensive recurrence in tumor relative to normal tissue (Figure 5A), indicating that assembly programs associated with epithelial plasticity are shared across epithelial cancer lineages. To identify assembly systems most strongly linked to disease progression, we asked which lesion-associated proteomic changes converged with recurrent genomic alterations in gastroesophageal tumors (Figure 5D; Table S10). Strikingly, several highest-ranking convergent assemblies contained endolysosomal or lipid-trafficking components. In addition, complementary functional enrichment identified endomembrane and endolysosomal systems among dynamic assemblies (adjusted P < 0.005; Figure 5E), reinforcing intracellular trafficking as a prominent axis of assembly remodeling during epithelial plasticity.
Mapping these disease-associated dynamic assemblies onto cellular architecture revealed coordinated remodeling of Exocyst, Retromer/SNX, ESCRT-II and lysosomal systems, together with junctional and actin-associated machinery (Figure 5F). This convergence prioritized specific endolysosomal interactions, including MON1B–ZNF143 and SH3KBP1–IFT27, for direct interrogation in human tissue.
Together, these findings show that experimentally defined hybrid assembly programs recur across epithelial cancers and converge on endolysosomal trafficking during Barrett’s progression, nominating this machinery as a candidate physical link between epithelial plasticity and the evolving tissue environment.
Endolysosomal interactions mark immune tissue organization and Barrett’s progression
Having identified endolysosomal trafficking as a major axis of hybrid-state assembly remodeling (Figure 5), we asked whether complexome-nominated endolysosomal protein pairs could be detected directly in human tissue. Because endolysosomal trafficking regulates cell-surface and secretory programs that shape epithelial–immune interactions (Néel et al. 2024), we further asked whether these signals were associated with local immune organization. We combined in situ proximity ligation assays (PLA), which detect paired proteins within ~40 nm directly in tissue, with multiplexed single-cell imaging across two independent clinical cohorts (Figure 6A). A discovery cohort of 26 FFPE specimens from eight patients spanning Barrett’s disease stages was profiled by 27-plex mIHC, while an independent outcome cohort comprised baseline surveillance biopsies from 18 patients followed for a median of 6.2 years, ten with indolent BE and eight who subsequently progressed to HGD or EAC.
Neoplastic progression was accompanied by remodeling of immune composition, including expansion of regulatory T cells and dendritic cells in dysplastic and tumor tissue (Figure 6B). In situ PLA directly detected the complexome-nominated MON1B–ZNF143 and SH3KBP1–IFT27 proximity signals in dysplastic Barrett’s mucosa (Figure 6C). To explore local immune relationships within native tissue architecture, we evaluated adjacent serial sections from a discovery dysplasia specimen across matched mucosal regions of interest (ROIs; Figure 6D). This analysis revealed two distinct, complementary modes of microenvironmental remodeling: MON1B–ZNF143 PLA signal correlated positively with immunoregulatory cell density (Spearman ρ = +0.65 across n = 14 ROIs), reflecting an association with a tolerogenic stroma enriched for resident monocytes and regulatory T cells (mature CD68⁺/CD163⁺ macrophages were below detection threshold in these mucosal crops). Conversely, SH3KBP1–IFT27 PLA signal correlated inversely with local immune infiltration (Spearman ρ = −0.51 across n = 19 ROIs), demarcating an immune-depleted epithelial niche. These patterns indicated that hybrid-state endolysosomal assemblies associate with locally altered immune microenvironments, either through tolerogenic immune infiltration or immune exclusion, motivating multiscale, patient-level evaluation in an independent cohort.
To test whether these spatial couplings generalize across patients at paracrine resolution, we evaluated baseline surveillance biopsies from an independent, outcome-annotated cohort (18 independent patient cores; Figure 6E–G) across a defined 50-µm spatial scale. Cytotoxic and regulatory lymphoid lineages (CD45, CD3, CD4, CD8, CD20, and FOXP3) were consistently excluded from the local microenvironment of both endolysosomal complexes (Figure 6H). In particular, SH3KBP1–IFT27 exhibited significant local depletion of CD3 (partial Spearman ρ = −0.096, P = 0.002) and CD8 (partial ρ = −0.087, P = 0.011), confirming at paracrine resolution the immune-depleted niche observed in discovery tissue. Myeloid markers displayed pair-specific couplings: CD68 showed modest positive associations with both assemblies, whereas CD163 associated positively with MON1B–ZNF143 but inversely with SH3KBP1–IFT27 (Figure 6H), mirroring the tolerogenic versus immune-cold microenvironments identified in Figure 6D. Activation and checkpoint markers (including CD103, Granzyme B, and HLA-II) similarly trended negative at 50 µm. Together, these spatial patterns indicate that hybrid endolysosomal assemblies demarcate a locally suppressed or immune-excluded microenvironment depleted of infiltrating, functional cytotoxic effectors.
We next asked whether these endolysosomal proximity signals track with clinical progression in this surveillance cohort (8 progressors vs. 10 non-progressors). In baseline biopsies, progressor lesions displayed a trend toward higher median PLA density, with MON1B–ZNF143 and SH3KBP1–IFT27 exhibiting 1.52- and 1.43-fold higher geometric median densities, respectively, relative to non-progressors (Figure 6I; ratios reflect geometric medians of log-transformed counts). Progressor tissues also showed higher group-median Ki-67, αSMA, CD68, and CD163 signals (Figure 6I). While these exploratory observations warrant validation in larger clinical cohorts, the convergence of local immune exclusion and elevated assembly signals in aggressive lesions provided the direct impetus to functionally test whether epithelial endolysosomal machinery directly impairs T-cell activation in Figure 7.
Together, these independent cohorts establish tissue detection and immune spatial context for complexome-nominated trafficking proximity signals, with higher median baseline PLA densities observed in surveillance patients who subsequently progressed. We therefore asked whether perturbing this trafficking machinery directly alters epithelial–immune communication.
Epithelial endolysosomal machinery shapes T-cell responses
The spatial observation that network-nominated endolysosomal assemblies demarcate immune-excluded microenvironments depleted of infiltrating T cells prompted us to test whether this machinery functionally influences T-cell responses. We focused on both MON1B and SH3KBP1 as representative regulatory nodes within the endolysosomal trafficking program, based on their established roles in endosomal maturation and trafficking (Janz et al. 2026; Soubeyran et al. 2002; Yasuda et al. 2016) and their prioritization by the patient-convergent networks. SH3KBP1 abundance increased across Barrett’s lesion stages, whereas MON1B was prioritized through complexome rewiring rather than differential-abundance screening (Table S1). This contrast enabled us to test whether interaction-network remodeling could reveal functional regulators beyond abundance-based discovery.
To functionally test these regulators in an epithelial cancer lineage showing recurrence of the assembly program, we used A549 cells, a well-established model of TGFβ-induced plasticity (Kasai et al. 2005) that is amenable to CRISPR perturbation and expresses EphA2, enabling bispecific T-cell engagers (BiTEs) to recruit T cells directly to target cells. We generated MON1B- and SH3KBP1-knockout lines and compared parental and TGFβ-conditioned targets in co-cultures with PBMCs and purified T cells. TGFβ-conditioned wild-type (WT) cells shifted CD4⁺ T cells away from the CD69-high activation states induced by unconditioned controls, whereas deletion of either MON1B or SH3KBP1 partially reversed this effect (Figure 7A). Multivariate analysis of 12 CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ features in purified T-cell experiments similarly separated responses to unconditioned and TGFβ-conditioned WT targets along an axis dominated by CD69 and CD25 (Figure 7B). Profiles generated by either knockout shifted away from the TGFβ-conditioned WT state, and direct comparisons confirmed recovery of CD69 and CD25 across multiple T-cell compartments (Figure 7C). 
Independent CyTOF profiling reinforced the link between hybrid-state endolysosomal remodeling and T-cell function, with intracellular cytokine expression reduced in T cells exposed to TGFβ-conditioned WT cells but increased following MON1B or SH3KBP1 deletion (Figure 7D). Because MON1B and SH3KBP1 regulate complementary aspects of late-endosomal/lysosomal trafficking and receptor internalization, respectively, we asked whether this effect extended beyond BiTE-dependent activation. Following CD3/CD28 stimulation, co-cultures with MON1B- or SH3KBP1-knockout epithelial targets showed larger cytokine-positive fractions than co-cultures with TGFβ-conditioned WT targets (Figure 7E,F). MON1B-knockout co-cultures showed a lower Granzyme B⁺CD107a⁺ fraction and a higher TNFα-positive fraction than TGFβ-conditioned WT co-cultures at the measured timepoint. Thus, perturbing complexome-nominated endolysosomal regulators altered T-cell activation and co-culture cytokine/degranulation profiles. 
Together, these complementary co-culture experiments support a model in which the complexome-nominated regulators MON1B and SH3KBP1 influence epithelial–immune communication, linking disease-associated trafficking machinery to T-cell activation and co-culture response composition (Figure 7G), providing a functional link between disease-associated endolysosomal assembly states and T-cell responses.

DISCUSSION
Patient-matched profiling across the BE–HGD–EAC continuum identified persistent epithelial–mesenchymal plasticity during neoplastic progression, prompting us to ask how such hybrid states are physically organized. Native complexome mapping across Barrett’s-derived and temporal epithelial models revealed extensive reorganization of protein assemblies that was not apparent from protein abundance alone. Most notably, intermediate-hybrid states exhibited a distinct chimeric architecture integrating epithelial adhesion with cytoskeletal, trafficking, metabolic and stress-response machinery. These experimentally defined assembly programs were engaged across Barrett’s progression and multiple epithelial cancers, while spatial and functional analyses converged on endolysosomal trafficking as a link to altered epithelial–immune communication. Together, our findings identify dynamic macromolecular assembly as a physical organizing layer of epithelial plasticity, providing a mechanism by which cells can acquire hybrid functions through reorganization of existing molecular machinery and connecting molecular state to tissue behavior.
The intermediate-hybrid state was particularly distinctive. Rather than representing a midpoint between epithelial and mesenchymal endpoints, it contained transient combinations of machinery conventionally associated with different cellular programs. We use the term chimeric assembly architecture to describe this organization within connected physical networks, without implying that every co-complex association represents a direct binary contact. Such organization provides a physical basis for a defining feature of epithelial–mesenchymal plasticity: retention of epithelial organization alongside acquisition of migratory, stress-adaptive and other mesenchymal-associated capabilities (Yang et al. 2020; Marjanovic et al. 2020; Jolly et al. 2016). Orthogonal measurements supported selected interactions, while perturbation revealed state-selective functional vulnerabilities. Thus, whereas transcriptional and regulatory networks help specify and stabilize plastic cell states (Migault et al. 2022), assembly remodeling provides a complementary layer through which these states are physically executed.
This distinction extends conventional definitions of molecular state, which are dominated by transcript and protein abundance. Native complexome profiling instead resolves how proteins are organized into functional assemblies (Havugimana et al. 2022; Goel et al. 2024). Here, MON1B exemplifies the value of this organizational layer: interaction-network remodeling nominated it for functional testing beyond differential-abundance screening. Conservation between MCF10A and Barrett’s-derived CP-A interaction landscapes further enabled experimentally defined assembly programs to be projected into clinical specimens that cannot readily support native biochemical fractionation (McWhite et al. 2021). This experimental-to-clinical strategy uses experimentally resolved interaction maps as physical blueprints that can be projected into human disease, adding a macromolecular organizational dimension to transcriptomic, proteomic and spatial definitions of cellular state (Strasser et al. 2025).
Disease analyses further revealed that intermediate-hybrid assembly programs were enriched across Barrett’s progression and recurrent across multiple epithelial cancers. Within Barrett’s disease, complementary molecular analyses converged on endolysosomal and vesicular-trafficking machinery, systems positioned to translate intracellular state into receptor trafficking, membrane organization and extracellular communication (Yasuda et al. 2016; Soubeyran et al. 2002). Complexome-nominated MON1B–ZNF143 and SH3KBP1–IFT27 reporters detected in Barrett’s tissue demarcated local immune-excluded microenvironments characterized by relative depletion of cytotoxic T cells. In an independent surveillance cohort, both complexes displayed exploratory trends toward higher baseline abundance in progressor lesions within a broader proliferative, stromal and myeloid-associated tissue state. Targeted perturbation of representative endolysosomal regulators, MON1B or SH3KBP1, altered T-cell activation, cytokine production and effector-state composition in epithelial co-culture models. These observations suggest that remodeling of intracellular trafficking machinery may provide one route through which epithelial plasticity becomes coupled to the surrounding immune environment.
These conclusions should be interpreted within the inherent boundaries of current high-throughput interactomics and clinical sampling. While co-fractionation robustly charts soluble native assemblies, it captures stable multi-protein communities rather than direct binary contacts. Targeted structural modeling and orthogonal assays substantiate key associations, though resolving full complex architectures proteome-wide remains a future frontier. Similarly, because global complexome profiling demands protein inputs exceeding standard Barrett’s biopsy yields, temporal kinetics were mapped in TGFβ-driven models and projected into patient proteomes. Spatially, our reporter analyses in retrospective cohorts provide initial proof-of-concept, establishing the need for scaled, prospective multiplex imaging and proximity assays as longitudinal tissue banks expand. Genetically, MON1B and SH3KBP1 perturbations define node-level necessity, highlighting structure-guided interface mutagenesis as the natural next step to isolate interaction-specific causality. Together with reductionist co-culture models that simplify the full in vivo immune milieu, these findings establish endolysosomal remodeling as a compelling mechanistic axis linking epithelial plasticity to microenvironmental organization, delineating clear targets for targeted causal dissection.
The interaction networks, assembly programs, structural models and clinical projections are available through the EMT-InMap portal. Across complementary biochemical, structural, spatial and functional analyses, our results converge on a consistent picture: BE progression is characterized by persistent epithelial plasticity associated with extensive remodeling of native protein assemblies; hybrid states adopt distinct chimeric architectures; these assembly programs recur across human epithelial cancers; and endolysosomal remodeling connects this molecular organization to immune tissue context and function. These findings support a view of cellular plasticity in which state is encoded not only by molecular abundance, but also by the physical organization of proteins into functional assemblies. Dynamic assembly mapping therefore provides a molecular framework connecting protein organization, epithelial plasticity and tissue immune context, while nominating functional regulators beyond abundance-based descriptions of disease.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND SUBJECT DETAILS
Cell culture and EMT induction. Human breast epithelial MCF10A cells were kindly provided by Prof. Senthil Muthuswamy (National Cancer Institute, Bethesda). Cells were cultured in DMEM/F-12 supplemented with 5% Horse serum, 20 ng/mL EGF (Sigma), 10 μg/mL Insulin (Sigma), 0.5 mg/mL Hydrocortisone (Sigma), 100 ng/mL Cholera toxin (Sigma), 100 units/mL Penicillin and 100 μg/mL Streptomycin (HyClone) and grown at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. To induce EMT, MCF10A cells were stimulated with 10 ng/mL TGFβ (Invivogen). To ensure all samples were harvested at the same time, treatments were staggered across the time course. Replicate cell cultures were harvested at sub-confluence by scraping in 1x PBS. Cells were centrifuged at 4,000 ×g for 5 minutes, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until use.
A549 knockout target-cell models. A549 wild-type, MON1B-knockout and SH3KBP1-knockout cells were used for the BiTE and CyTOF co-culture experiments. Target cells were compared in untreated parental-state and TGFβ-conditioned states. A549 parental cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, CCL-185) and cultured in F-12K Medium (ATCC, 30-2004) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37°C with 5% CO₂. CRISPR-mediated knockouts of SH3KBP1 and MON1B were generated by ribonucleoprotein electroporation (1320 V, 30 ms, 2 pulses) into parental A549 cells using custom synthetic guide RNAs (Ubigene). For SH3KBP1, targeting gRNAs (gRNA1: AATGACAAAATGCTCGAAATTGG, gRNA2: TGCGTTTGCAAAAGCTATCAAGG) generated a homozygous 261-bp deletion across Exon 7 (clone #C6). For MON1B, targeting gRNAs (gRNA3: TGACTGAGAAGTCCGTGACATGG, gRNA4: GCACCTAGTGCGTCTCGCAGCGG) generated a homozygous 242-bp deletion across Exon 4 (clone #G12). Knockout clones were selected in 1 µg/mL puromycin and validated by PCR and Sanger sequencing. Parental and knockout lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and routinely confirmed negative for mycoplasma contamination (MycoAlert, Lonza).
Human esophageal tissue specimens. Human esophageal tissue specimens were obtained from Oregon Health and Science University Hospital under institutional review board (IRB) approved protocol [Protocol #1759 - Natural History of Esophageal Cancer and Related Diseases (ECRD)]. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study cohort included n = 27 patients undergoing endoscopic surveillance or surgical resection, covering the histological spectrum of non-dysplastic BE, HGD and EAC, each contributing one lesion and one patient-matched normal-adjacent tissue (54 specimens). Eligible patients had a confirmed histological diagnosis and sufficient tissue for proteomic analysis; patients who had received neoadjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy before sampling were excluded. Normal-adjacent tissue was defined as histologically unremarkable squamous or gastric-type mucosa sampled at least 2 cm from the lesion in the same endoscopic session and confirmed lesion-free by the reviewing pathologists. Specimens were processed in a randomized order with respect to stage; proteomic acquisition and primary data processing were performed blind to histological stage, whereas downstream statistical analysis was necessarily unblinded. Clinical diagnoses were confirmed by two independent board-certified pathologists. Fresh biopsies were collected, immediately snap-frozen on dry-ice and stored at -80°C until processing. All clinical metadata, including TNM staging and treatment history, were de-identified and maintained in a secure database.
Barrett’s tissue microarrays. hBE-TMA1 and hBE-TMA2 contained ten positions each, including one orientation position per array. METHOD DETAILS
Sample preparation and proteomic data acquisition
Preparation of native protein extracts. Cell lysis was performed by resuspension and Dounce homogenization in a Native lysis buffer containing 10 mM Tris-HCl, 250 mM sucrose, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 0.1% (v/v) dodecyl-β-D-maltopyranoside (DDM) and 1× cOmplete Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche). The homogenates were treated with Turbonuclease (100 units/mL) (Accelagen) for 30 minutes at 4°C, clarified by centrifugation at 18,000×g for 20 minutes at 4°C, quantified by Bradford assay (Bio-Rad) and adjusted to 2 mg protein/mL prior to fractionation.
Biochemical fractionation by ion-exchange HPLC. Clarified protein lysates from each sample were fractionated using an Agilent 1260 Infinity binary HPLC system consisting of a stacked assembly of a weak anion exchange PolyWAX LP column and a weak cation exchange PolyCAT A column. The columns were conditioned in buffer A (50 mM Ammonium acetate pH 7.5 and 3 mM NaN3) prior to loading 2 mg lysate. For each sample, ninety-six 500-μL fractions were collected into a 96-well plate at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min using a gradient elution of 0-67% buffer B (1.5M Ammonium acetate pH 7.5 and 3 mM NaN3) from 8-80 min, followed by 67-100% between 80-104 minutes. Protein elution was monitored by determining the UV absorbance at 280 nm. After collection, fractions were dried in a SpeedVac.
Protein denaturation, digestion, and TMT labeling. The dried protein pellets were resuspended in GuHCl lysis buffer (6 M Guanidine-HCl, 100 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.5, 10 mM TCEP, 40 mM CAA, 6 mM CaCl₂; 75 µL/well), incubated at 90°C for 25 min at 1,000 rpm, and cooled on ice for 10 min. The GuHCl concentration was diluted by adding 140 µL H₂O per well, followed by the addition of 300 µL of 100% ethanol and 100 µg of an equal 1:1 mixture of hydrophobic and hydrophilic Sera-Mag SpeedBead carboxylate-modified magnetic particles (GE Healthcare / Cytiva) per well. The binding mixture was shaken for 5 min at room temperature at 800 rpm and incubated on ice to promote protein–bead binding. Automated bead cleanup, washing, and on-bead digestion were performed using a KingFisher Flex Magnetic Particle Processor (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in a 96-well format. Briefly, magnetic beads with captured proteins were sequentially washed twice with 80% ethanol and once with 100% acetonitrile on the automated platform. Bound proteins were released into 150 µL of enzymatic digestion buffer per well (50 mM TEAB containing sequencing-grade modified trypsin at ~50 µg per plate; ~1:50 enzyme-to-protein ratio) and digested overnight at 37°C with continuous agitation at 550 rpm. Digested peptides were recovered, dried in a SpeedVac, and resuspended in a peptide labeling buffer (60% acetonitrile in 50 mM TEAB) at a volume of 70 µL per well. Tandem Mass Tag (TMT, Thermo Fisher Scientific) isobaric labeling was performed in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol, with optimized stoichiometric partitioning across fractions to minimize reagent consumption: each 96-well plate corresponding to a distinct TGFβ treatment time-point was labeled with an individual 5-mg TMT channel uniformly distributed across the wells. Corresponding elution fractions across all time-point plates were subsequently pooled into 96 multiplexed fractions, desalted, and dried under vacuum centrifugation prior to LC-MS/MS analysis.
Nanoflow LC-MS/MS data acquisition. TMT-labeled peptides, dissolved in mobile phase A (2% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid), were loaded onto a C18 trap column by the Easy-nLC 1200 HPLC system. They were subsequently separated on a 75 μm i.d × 50 cm EASY-spray column with a 90-minute gradient of mobile phase B (80% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid) at a flow rate of 250 nL/min, before injection into the Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer. The instrument was operated in positive ion mode with an electrospray voltage of 2.1 kV. High-energy collision dissociation (HCD) fragmentation data were collected in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode, with precursor ions (MS1, 300–1,500 m/z) scanned at a resolution of 120,000 at m/z 200. The injection time was set to 60 ms with an AGC target of 3 × 106 ions. The top 10 precursor ions were chosen for fragmentation (MS2) and scanned at a resolution of 45,000, with an injection time of 60 ms and an AGC target of 1 × 105 ions.
Primary data processing and PPI scoring
Primary data processing. The raw data underwent processing using MaxQuant (version 1.6.1.0) with the Andromeda search engine against a FASTA file of the Homo sapiens proteome (dated April 2019; 20,514 entries) obtained from UniprotKB. Variable modifications included oxidation of methionine and acetylation of protein N-terminals, while carbamidomethylation of cysteine was considered a fixed modification. For quantification, reporter ion MS2 with predefined 10-plex TMT parameters was used. Trypsin/P was chosen as the proteolytic enzyme, allowing for up to two missed cleavages. Match-between-runs were enabled across fractions within a replicate series only, with a 0.7 min match time window and a 20 min alignment time window. Manufacturer-provided TMT batch-specific isotope correction factors were applied within MaxQuant. Peptide-spectrum match, peptide and protein identification were each filtered at a 1% false-discovery rate estimated by target–decoy search. Reporter-ion quantification used a precursor intensity fraction filter of 0.75 to limit co-isolation interference, and only razor and unique peptides contributed to protein-level quantification.
Computational scoring of protein-protein interactions (PPIs). Protein groups flagged as reverse hits or potential contaminants were removed. Reporter intensities of zero were set to missing, and protein groups detected in only one fraction were removed. Intensities were log2 transformed and then rescaled within each replicate group to the global minimum–maximum range of the combined matrix, which places replicates on a common scale without imputing missing values; quantile normalization across fractions was evaluated as an alternative and gave concordant networks. Missing values were left missing throughout and were never imputed to zero; EPIC co-elution scores are computed on observed values only. The normalized matrix released as Table S5 is the exact input to co-elution scoring. The resulting matrix was processed using EPIC to predict PPIs using a random forest (RF) classifier with default parameters, as described previously (Hu et al. 2019). Briefly, co-elution scores between protein profiles were calculated using five established similarity measures: Euclidean, Bayes, Jaccard, Apex and Mutual Information. The RF classifier was trained and evaluated on a large set of positive reference “gold standard” protein complexes curated from IntAct and CORUM. A parallel negative set was created from randomized combinations of components assigned to distinct assemblies. To avoid bias, no functional evidence was used in the scoring scheme. Complexes in the training set sharing most subunits (fractional overlap >0.8) were merged. The best models were then merged to generate the final probabilistic co-complex relationships, called EMT-iMap.
Assembly detection and temporal-program assignment
ClusterONE assembly detection. The weighted EPIC network was analyzed with ClusterONE v1.0 using density = 0.3, minimum size = 2, maximum overlap = 0.8, single-linkage merging and node-based seeding. Detected communities were ranked by size and converted to long-form complex-membership and GMT files. This workflow yielded 611 assemblies (Table S4). Because ClusterONE permits overlapping communities, a protein could occur in more than one assembly.
Assembly GSVA and temporal pattern assignment. Assembly gene sets were scored on the collapsed five-state mCF/MS matrix using GSVA with the gsvaParam implementation and default kernel settings (GSVA package). For each assembly, scores across day 0, 2, 4, 6 and 8 were row-standardized and correlated by Pearson correlation with three fixed templates: increasing (0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1), decreasing (1, 0.75, 0.5, 0.25, 0) and intermediate-peaking (0, 0.5, 1, 0.5, 0). Assemblies were assigned to the best-matching pattern when r ≥ 0.70; otherwise they were unclassified. Decreasing, intermediate-peaking and increasing groups were termed epithelial-legacy (EL; n = 82), intermediate-hybrid (IH; n = 62) and mesenchymal-emerging (ME; n = 91), respectively (Table S6).
Hierarchical annotation and spatial mapping
GOCC database and DAG construction. To facilitate hierarchical spatial mapping, we developed a custom annotation framework. We utilized a Python-based recursive depth-first search (DFS) to traverse the Gene Ontology (GO) basic OBO (v2025-01-01) and extract all root-to-leaf paths within the Cellular Component (CC) Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG; GO:0005575). This hierarchy was merged with human-specific gene annotations from the EBI Gene Ontology Annotation (GOA) database (HUMAN/goa_human.gaf) to create a comprehensive mapping between gene symbols and their respective hierarchical GOCC positions.
Over-representation analysis (ORA) of assemblies. Statistical enrichment of the protein assemblies was performed using the ‘enricher’ function from the clusterProfiler R package (v4.5). For each predicted assembly, we calculated the over-representation of GOCC terms using a hypergeometric test. The background "universe" was strictly limited to the set of proteins represented within the GOCC database to ensure statistical conservativeness. Significant terms were identified using a p-value and q-value cutoff of 0.05. Multiple testing corrections were applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method. We set a minimum gene set size (minGSSize) of 3 to ensure the biological relevance of the enriched terms.
Spatial compartment assignment and treemap generation. To resolve the large-scale spatial organization of the multiprotein complexes, enriched GOCC terms were assigned to high-level cellular compartments (e.g., Nucleus, Mitochondrion, Plasma Membrane) using a keyword-matching heuristic. Assemblies were assigned based on the hierarchical `level2_term` derived from our custom DAG or through manual curation of the `last_go_term`. Data were visualized as a hierarchical treemap using the treemap R package. Tile area represents complex size (number of subunits), while color intensity represents the adjusted P-value (–log₁₀).
Biochemical and fluorescence validation of nominated pairs
Co-immunoprecipitation and immunoblotting. Selected CFMS-nominated pairs were tested in MCF10A lysates by co-immunoprecipitation of the indicated bait followed by immunoblot detection of its nominated partner. Bead-only, input and immunoprecipitated fractions were run in parallel. TBC1D5–RAB7A was assessed at days 0, 4 and 8 after TGFβ exposure; the remaining pairs were tested under the conditions shown in Figure 4B.
Co-immunoprecipitation and confocal immunofluorescence. For co-immunoprecipitation, cell lysates (1.5 mg total protein input) were prepared in lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, and protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Roche). Lysates were incubated with 2 µg of anti-MON1B (Proteintech 24508-1-AP) or anti-SH3KBP1 (Santa Cruz sc-271376) antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by capture with 30 µL of Protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher) for 2 hours. Beads were washed three times in the wash buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP-40) and eluted in SDS loading buffer for immunoblotting (n = 3 independent experiments). For confocal immunofluorescence, MCF10A cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 20 minutes, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 minutes, and blocked with 3% BSA in PBS for 1 hour. Primary antibodies included E-cadherin (mouse mAb, CST 14472, 1:200) and Vimentin (rabbit mAb, CST 5741, 1:400) incubated overnight at 4°C, followed by Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse and Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (1:500, Thermo Fisher). Coverslips were mounted using Duolink Mounting Medium with DAPI and imaged on a Zeiss LSM 980 confocal microscope using a 63x/1.4 NA oil immersion objective (n = 3 independent cell preparations).
Literature-derived assembly motifs: Literature-derived relationships between protein assemblies were represented as a directed graph, with duplicate edges and self-links removed and no distinction between activating and inhibitory effects. Feed-forward loops, convergent inputs, reciprocal pairs and directed cycles were compared with randomized graphs preserving each assembly’s incoming and outgoing connection counts. Additional controls randomized gene-level relationships before reconstructing the assembly graph. Two-sided Monte Carlo comparisons used Holm correction across all four motif counts, although convergent-input counts were fixed by the preserved incoming connection counts. Overlap with temporally regulated assemblies used the authoritative temporal-membership table (Table S6) and an exploratory hypergeometric test against the complete assembly universe, without matching for connectivity. 
Clinical projection and patient cohort analysis
Acquisition of BE-HGD-EAC patient proteomics: Briefly, proteins were extracted from tissue specimens using a guanidine hydrochloride (GuHCl)-based lysis buffer (6 M GuHCl, 100 mM Tris pH 8.5, 10 mM TCEP, 40 mM CAA) coupled with probe sonication. Following denaturation and clarification, proteins were subjected to a dual digestion with Lys-C (1:100 w/w, 4 hours) and trypsin (1:50 w/w, overnight). The resulting peptides were desalted using C18 SPE and 75 µg peptides per sample were labeled with TMTpro 18-plex reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The combined multiplexed sample was fractionated using high-pH reversed-phase chromatography, where 96 initial fractions were concatenated into 24 final fractions. Each fraction was analyzed on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer over a 120-minute gradient. The instrument was operated in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode, acquiring MS1 scans at 120,000 resolution and HCD-generated MS2 scans at 45,000 resolution for the top 12 precursor ions.
Bulk proteomics data normalization and processing: Proteins exhibiting >30% missingness across samples were excluded. Global signal normalization was achieved using variance stabilizing normalization (VSN). Missing values were imputed via local least squares (LLS) regression. Outlier samples were removed prior to downstream modeling. Batch correction was applied to adjust for technical covariates such as TMT plex.
Complex-level enrichment via gene set variation analysis (GSVA): Complex-level enrichment scores were computed using the GSVA R package (Hänzelmann et al. 2013).
Differential protein abundance analysis. Stage-specific differential abundance was analyzed with limma using within-patient lesion-minus-NAT log₂ abundance differences from complete matched pairs. The design estimated separate mean differences for BE, HGD and EAC, with independent within-pair TMT-plex indicator differences included to account for cross-plex pairs. Statistical inference used robust empirical Bayes moderation, with Benjamini–Hochberg correction across all tested proteins within each stage. Proteins were classified as differentially abundant at |log₂ fold change| ≥ 0.6 and adjusted P < 0.05. Each patient contributed one paired difference and constituted the independent biological unit.
Hallmark pathway analysis. Human MSigDB Hallmark gene sets were used to compute GSVA scores. Lesion and matched NAT scores were compared across the 20 complete patient pairs using two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, with Benjamini–Hochberg correction across all 50 pathways.
Patient-matched EMT marker heatmap. Measured epithelial and mesenchymal signature proteins from Mak et al. (Mak et al. 2016) were selected for display if they met FDR < 0.01 and |log₂ fold change| ≥ 0.5 in at least one stage-specific lesion-versus-NAT contrast. Heatmap values represent within-patient lesion-minus-NAT log₂ abundance differences without row standardization. Proteins were grouped by epithelial or mesenchymal annotation and ordered within each group by mean absolute fold change across the three stage contrasts. Patients were grouped by histological stage and ordered within each stage by the smaller of their epithelial-retention and mesenchymal-activation fractions, calculated from all measured signature members using the thresholds defined in the legend.
Epithelial and mesenchymal signature scoring. The epithelial and mesenchymal gene sets from Mak et al. (Mak et al. 2016) were scored separately using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA; GSEApy) on the processed clinical proteome matrix. Entries mapping to the same gene symbol were averaged, and genes with remaining missing values or zero variance were excluded. Scoring used within-sample ranks and all eligible measured signature members, without restricting inputs to proteins selected for the heatmap. For each signature, enrichment scores were standardized by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation of NAT scores within the corresponding histological stage. Complete patient-matched NAT–lesion pairs were displayed in Figure 1G.
Somatic-alteration context. Gene-level alteration frequencies for stomach, esophageal and pan-cancer cohorts were obtained from cBioPortal and summarized across genes in each assembly (Table S10). These cohort-level summaries provide genomic context for nominated modules and do not establish assembly formation or activity in individual tumors.
Multiplex IHC on BE–HGD–EAC specimens. Multiplex immunohistochemistry was performed on 5-μm FFPE sections with a 27-antibody panel as described previously (Sivagnanam et al. 2023; Banik et al. 2020; Tsujikawa et al. 2017). The analyzed dataset comprised 26 FFPE blocks from eight patients and 289 regions of interest, yielding 3,983,670 classified single cells across disease stages. Single cells were phenotyped using the marker logic in Figure S5C. Sankey plots were generated from cell-density summaries with the alluvial package in R. Heatmaps show Z-scored median densities. Population differences across normal, combined metaplasia/dysplasia and tumor groups were screened with Kruskal–Wallis tests followed, when significant, by Dunn pairwise tests with Bonferroni adjustment. Cells are nested measurements; stage-level interpretation is based on specimen/ROI summaries rather than treating individual cells as biological replicates.
Proximity ligation assay imaging and spatial immune-neighborhood analysis
In Situ proximity ligation assay (PLA). In situ PLA was performed using the Duolink In Situ Red Starter Kit Mouse/Rabbit (Sigma-Aldrich, DUO92101). Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections from high-grade dysplasia blocks (block TO24-00109) were deparaffinized and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 20 minutes in a pressure cooker. Sections were blocked and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies: anti-MON1B (rabbit pAb, Proteintech, #24508-1-AP, 1:100) and anti-ZNF143 (mouse mAb, Santa Cruz sc-100983, 1:100); or anti-SH3KBP1 (rabbit pAb, CST 12248, 1:100) and anti-IFT27 (mouse mAb, Santa Cruz sc-376510, 1:50). PLA probes (anti-rabbit PLUS and anti-mouse MINUS) were applied for 1 hour at 37°C, followed by ligation and amplification at 37°C. Sections were mounted with Duolink Mounting Medium with DAPI and imaged on a Zeiss LSM 980 confocal microscope. Technical controls (omission of one primary antibody) showed minimal background signal (< 0.1 puncta/cell).
PLA image processing. In situ PLA was performed in dysplastic Barrett’s tissue for selected pairs, including MON1B–ZNF143 and SH3KBP1–IFT27. Image stacks were converted to maximum-intensity projections. For display in Figure 6C, the PLA channel was normalized, thresholded by Otsu’s method, filtered to remove objects smaller than three pixels and rendered in magenta with a sixfold intensity boost and four-pixel morphological dilation; DAPI was rendered in blue. These operations were used only to make puncta visible in the representative montage and were not used for the quantitative association in Figure 6D.
Spatial PLA–immune neighborhood analysis in the mIHC cohort. To evaluate the local spatial relationship between endolysosomal complexes and infiltrating immune populations, PLA and multiplexed IHC were performed on adjacent 4-µm serial tissue sections from patient BE11. Regions of interest (ROIs) were matched across serial sections using landmark-guided alignment of mucosal glandular contours. Because serial 4-µm sections exhibit microscopic differences in cellular plane and local glandular geometry that preclude single-cell co-registration without elastic deformation, analyses were integrated across the physical boundaries of each matched mucosal ROI (mean ROI area = 2.81 mm², range 0.51–3.99 mm²). Although Duolink In Situ Red reagents fluoresce in the orange-red spectrum (~594/624 nm), discovery fluorescence was captured using far-red optics recorded as 'AF647' in native microscope metadata. PLA signal was quantified as mean pixel intensity (a.u.) within each ROI. Immunoregulatory cell density was evaluated within the same physical ROI boundaries for MON1B–ZNF143 (n = 14 ROIs) and SH3KBP1–IFT27 (n = 19 ROIs). In these mucosal crops, mature CD68⁺/CD163⁺ macrophages were below detection threshold (mean = 0.0 cells/mm² for MON1B; present in 4 of 19 ROIs for SH3KBP1, mean = 0.2 cells/mm²). Local immunoregulatory density was therefore summarized from resident monocytes and CD4⁺ regulatory T cells. Associations between mean PLA intensity and immunoregulatory cell density were assessed using Spearman rank correlation. These correlations describe exploratory patterns across serial sections without nominal P-values or patient-level generalization.
Regional PLA–immune-marker analysis in the outcome-annotated Lunaphore cohort. Regional associations between endolysosomal assembly density (MON1B–ZNF143 and SH3KBP1–IFT27) and multiplexed immune profiles were quantified across 18 independent patient surveillance cores (8 progressors, 10 non-progressors; one core per patient representing the independent biological unit). Analyses focused strictly on a defined 50-µm spatial scale to capture short-range, paracrine and cell-contact microenvironments. Serial-section PLA detections were coregistered into the 20-plex Lunaphore coordinate frame using high-precision VALIS non-rigid elastic transformations, with measurements restricted to the mutual intersection of moving and reference tissue masks. Within each 50-µm region, PLA density was calculated as qualified detections per unit tissue support area. Marker measurements from the 20-plex cyclic immunofluorescence panel were log1p-transformed and averaged across resident cells. To isolate specific immune couplings from confounding tissue architecture, partial Spearman rank correlations were computed within each core between PLA density and marker signal, controlling for cell density, observed support area, and cytokeratin (PANCK) intensity as an epithelial covariate. One partial correlation coefficient was extracted per core, protein pair, and marker across 17 immune targets: Lymphoid (CD45, CD3, CD4, CD8, CD20, FOXP3), Myeloid (CD68, CD163, CD11B, CD11C, CCR2), and Activation / Checkpoint markers (CD103, CD69, Granzyme B, HLA-II, PD-1, PD-L1). Mean effect sizes across the 18 biological cores and 95% bootstrap confidence intervals are displayed as a lineage-grouped forest plot alongside an aligned marker-resolved heatmap on a shared zero-centered color scale (−0.11 to +0.11). These analyses quantify patient-level spatial covariation between endolysosomal assembly density and immune profiles across registered serial sections.
Barrett’s TMA cyclic-immunofluorescence imaging
TMA cohort and Lunaphore sequential IF. The outcome-annotated Barrett’s esophagus tissue microarrays (hBE-TMA1 and hBE-TMA2) comprised 18 analyzable cores from 18 distinct patients (10 non-progressors, 8 progressors; orientation positions excluded). Sections were profiled using an automated sequential multiplex immunofluorescence platform (Lunaphore Discovery) with a 20-marker immune and tissue panel: CD163, CD68, αSMA, PanCK, HLA-II, CD11b, CCR2, CD11c, CD45, Ki-67, CD3, CD4, CD8, FOXP3, CD69, PD-L1, PD-1, Granzyme B, CD20, and CD103, with repeated DAPI.
Image segmentation and tissue masking. Single-cell Single-cell segmentation was performed on whole-slide OME-TIFFs, yielding 183,482 segmented single cells across the 18 analyzable patient cores. Marker intensities were quantified within nuclei and a 5-pixel perinuclear ring. To avoid edge and fragmentation artifacts from archival biopsy geometry, analyzable tissue area was derived from the DAPI channel via Gaussian smoothing, Otsu thresholding, binary closing, and hole filling. Cell phenotypes were assigned using outcome-blind gating prior to merging clinical annotations.
Core-level progression comparisons and statistical inference. The patient core was the independent biological unit, comprising eight progressor and ten non-progressor cores (one core per patient). Marker measurements were calculated as mean log1p-transformed raw signals within each core. CycIF contrasts were expressed as the exponential of the difference between progressor and non-progressor group medians of these core-level means. PLA density was calculated as supported puncta per mm² of observed registered tissue area, and contrasts are reported as ratios of group geometric medians: exp(medianₚ − medianₙₚ) on log-transformed counts, rather than linear intensity fold changes. Between-group differences were evaluated using exact two-sided Mann–Whitney rank-sum tests and 20,000-draw stratified percentile-bootstrap 95% confidence intervals: for MON1B–ZNF143, median ratio was 1.52× (95% CI [0.25, 2.95], P = 0.90); for SH3KBP1–IFT27, median ratio was 1.43× (95% CI [0.49, 3.52], P = 0.57). In pre-specified sensitivity analyses normalizing PLA counts to resident cell count (puncta per 1,000 cells), median ratios remained elevated at 1.28× (95% CI [0.32, 3.03], P = 0.97) for MON1B–ZNF143 and 1.14× (95% CI [0.41, 3.51], P = 0.70) for SH3KBP1–IFT27. Because confidence intervals crossed unity, these contrasts are presented as descriptive pilot summaries warranting validation in larger cohorts.
Serial-section PLA co-registration and regional spatial associations. PLA detection used a supervised random-forest classifier trained on manual point-and-click annotations of true puncta, artifacts, and background in native-resolution images. Puncta were treated as positive training examples, whereas artifacts and background were treated as negative examples. After deduplication, the training dataset contained 427 labelled candidates—140 positive and 287 negative examples across multiple scene groups. Multiscale Laplacian-of-Gaussian detection generated candidate spots for whole-core analysis, and the frozen classifier retained candidates using a probability cutoff of 0.905 calibrated on scene-grouped out-of-fold predictions. The probability cutoff of 0.905 was calibrated via scene-grouped out-of-fold cross-validation across the 427 annotated candidates, achieving 69.3% recall (97 of 140 true puncta) with zero false-positive calls among 287 negative examples (under a pre-specified ≥65% minimum recall policy). While out-of-fold partitioning prevents simple re-substitution bias, this metric reflects internal cross-validation rather than an independent external held-out test cohort. The negative-control annotation audit yielded one positive call among 142 annotated control points. Qualified punctum coordinates and native detection-support masks were transferred into the Lunaphore reference frame using recorded coordinate mappings and saved VALIS non-rigid transforms. Measurements were restricted to the intersection of fixed-section DAPI tissue, registered moving-section DAPI tissue, and transferred native PLA detection support. Locations outside this observed support were excluded. Detector support coverage and section quality were verified to show no systematic bias between outcome groups (median supported cell fraction 95.4% in progressors vs. 98.0% in non-progressors, Mann–Whitney P = 0.068; median tissue support area ratio 1.24×, bootstrap 95% CI [1.12, 1.79]). Local associations were evaluated in physical regions of 50, 100, and 200 µm width. PLA density was calculated as supported puncta per observed support area, and marker measurements were averaged within regions after log1p transformation of raw signals. Within each core, regional associations were estimated using partial Spearman correlations adjusted for Lunaphore cell count, observed support area, and PANCK signal. One effect was retained per core, PPI pair, spatial scale, and marker, and marker-level displays summarized mean effects across cores. The separate Tribus analysis evaluated its hard-called T-cell state against PLA density using unadjusted and adjusted within-core correlations, with the same covariates used for adjustment. 
Structural modeling of the interactome
Deep-learning-based structural prediction of PPIs. EPIC-derived pairs were modeled with an ensemble comprising PyTorchAF-2024 and AlphaFold-Multimer v2/v3 (Evans et al. 2022). PyTorchAF-2024 is an in-house AlphaFold2 implementation retrained on data available through 23 January 2023. Multiple-sequence alignments were generated with MMseqs2 (Steinegger and Söding 2017) against ColabFoldDB (Mirdita et al. 2022). Five predictions were generated per pair per model. Interfaces were scored with an interface-contact predicted TM metric using residue pairs with atoms within 4.5 Å, analogous to AF2Complex scoring (Gao et al. 2022). Each model was also run on 3,000 random pairs; model-specific score bins were converted to signal-to-noise ratios. High-confidence models were selected at signal-to-noise ≥ 3, corresponding approximately to interface-score cutoffs of 0.75 for AlphaFold-Multimer v2 and 0.80 for AlphaFold-Multimer v3/PyTorchAF-2024. This yielded 537 high-confidence pairs, including 232 without a corresponding PDB interface (Table S8; Figure S3).
Mapping of phosphorylation-mediated protein interactions. To identify EMT-associated phosphorylation-mediated protein interactions, we combined computational modeling with CFMS data and previously published phosphoproteomics data obtained in a related EMT study (Paul et al. 2023). Candidate phosphorylation-dependent interactions were modeled with Phospho-Tune (Glukhov et al. 2024). Multiple-sequence alignments were generated for phosphoprotein-binding-domain-containing partners with MMseqs2, and five Phospho-Tune models were run with six seeds. Models were screened for global confidence (mean pLDDT > 85) and local confidence around the modified residue (phosphosite or ±4-residue neighborhood pLDDT > 80). Table S9 contains the model-level phosphosite atlas. This analysis yielded 138 distinct pairs–phosphosite hypotheses across 93 phosphopeptides.
EMT-assembly blueprint projection into clinical proteomes
To ask whether the assembly programs defined by native complexome profiling (Figures 2,3) are also detectable in sample-limited clinical biopsies, we projected the corresponding fixed gene sets onto quantitative patient proteomes using single-sample gene set variation analysis (GSVA), an established enrichment method applied identically to the experimental reference and clinical domains. The reference domain comprised the five-state TGFβ-induced EMT series (Day 0, 2, 4, 6, 8), from which 235 dynamically regulated assemblies were classified into epithelial-legacy (EL), intermediate-hybrid (IH), and mesenchymal-emerging (ME) programs (Figure 3C). The clinical (target) domain comprised deep TMT proteomes from Barrett's esophagus (BE), high-grade dysplasia (HGD), esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC), and matched normal-adjacent tissue (NAT).
Identifier harmonization and shared gene universe. Protein identifiers from the reference and clinical datasets were harmonized to a common gene-symbol space, retaining genes that were finite and non-constant in both domains. Assemblies were retained for projection only if at least four member subunits fell within this shared, measurable gene universe, yielding 131 eligible assemblies (EL, n = 38; IH, n = 36; ME, n = 57).
GSVA scoring. For each domain, single-sample GSVA scores were computed independently for each eligible assembly using a Gaussian kernel (minSize = 1), and rescaled to the [0,1] interval, (score + 1)/2. Reference and clinical domains were scored separately and are not calibrated to a common absolute scale. Program-level scores (EL, IH, ME) were calculated as the equal-weighted mean of rescaled GSVA scores across all eligible assemblies in that program, giving each assembly equal influence regardless of size.
Hybrid Balance Score. The primary composite metric was the Hybrid Balance Score.
HBS = IH − |EL − ME|
The HBS quantifies hybrid program enrichment conditioned on balanced co-activation of epithelial and mesenchymal programs. Positive HBS values denote a true balanced hybrid assembly state where IH enrichment exceeds endpoint divergence. Conversely, negative HBS values reflect strong polarization toward either a unilineage epithelial (EL > ME) or mesenchymal (ME > EL) endpoint, where the magnitude of endpoint imbalance outweighs intermediate-hybrid program activity.
Robustness to scoring convention. Sensitivity analyses compared the primary assembly-mean scoring convention with GSVA of program-union gene sets and mean within-sample percentile ranks of those gene sets.
Cross-cancer generalization (CPTAC). The 45-assembly row set displayed in Figure 5A comprised IH and ME assemblies (26 and 19, respectively) that were independently significant in at least one stage-specific comparison: BE (7 NAT and 7 lesion specimens), HGD (8 NAT and 9 lesion specimens), or EAC (7 NAT and 9 lesion specimens), using unpaired limma moderated t-tests with Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted P < 0.05 and a positive lesion-minus-NAT GSVA score difference. To ask whether this same fixed assembly set was regulated beyond Barrett's neoplasia, all 45 assemblies were scored identically in five carcinomas profiled by the CPTAC pan-cancer proteogenomics consortium (colon adenocarcinoma [COAD], head and neck squamous carcinoma [HNSCC], lung squamous carcinoma [LSCC], lung adenocarcinoma [LUAD], and ovarian carcinoma [OV]). Within each CPTAC cohort, tumor and normal assembly scores were compared using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Assemblies were displayed where adjusted P < 0.05 and the median tumor score exceeded the median normal score; other cells were left blank. Barrett’s columns used the limma comparisons described above. Independently significant assemblies per column (of 45): COAD, 34; HNSCC, 32; LSCC, 24; LUAD, 26; OV, 28; pooled Barrett's (BE+HGD+EAC combined, 22 NAT and 25 lesion specimens), 32; BE, 39; HGD, 23; EAC, 4.
Statistical analysis. Within-patient NAT-versus-lesion comparisons of HBS and IH (Figure 5B,C) used two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, evaluated pooled across all patients (BE+HGD+EAC combined, n = 20 complete pairs) and within each histological stage. P values were Benjamini-Hochberg-corrected across the family of tests comprising both metrics, the three scoring conventions and the four analysis strata spanning both metrics (HBS, IH), the primary and two alternative scoring conventions, and four strata (pooled, BE, HGD, EAC).
Dynamic PPI volatility and functional perturbation analyses
Quantification of dynamic PPI volatility using BIONIC. To quantify temporal changes in inferred interaction rankings, we applied a rank-aware volatility framework to BIONIC predictions (Forster et al. 2022). Interaction volatility was defined as the mean absolute pairwise rank shift across the sampled EMT network states (Table S7).
Phenotypic small-molecule screen. AreaShape_Eccentricity (a geometric metric defined as the ratio of the distance between the foci of the fitted cell ellipse to its major axis length, ranging from 0 for a circular fitted ellipse toward 1 as the ellipse becomes increasingly elongated) measurements from untreated and TGFβ conditions were analyzed by two-way ANOVA for condition, drug and their interaction, followed by Tukey HSD comparisons. Adjusted P values compared drug-treated TGFβ conditions with TGFβ-only controls and unstimulated controls to assess morphological responses relative to the induced and baseline states. Scores were displayed as −log₁₀ adjusted P values (Figure 4D).
Live-cell motility assay and track quantification. MCF10A cells stimulated with recombinant human EGF (20 ng/mL) or TGFβ (10 ng/mL) were treated with 200 nM Hesperadin (SelleckChem, Cat# S1451) or vehicle control. Time-lapse phase-contrast images were acquired on an IncuCyte S3 platform using a 10× objective. The representative tracking overlays in Figure 4E correspond to 1, 24 and 72 hours, whereas the quantitative MSD analysis uses the 30–360-minute tracking window (Figure 4E). Image stacks were registered in Fiji, segmented using Cellpose (v2.2), and individual cell trajectories were reconstructed with BaxterAlgorithms.
Mean squared displacement (MSD) was computed across twelve 30-min intervals spanning a pre-specified window of 30–360 min and averaged per tracked cell after grouping by object identifier, ligand condition, treatment, and field of view. Statistical significance of Hesperadin-mediated motility inhibition was evaluated using two-sided Welch’s t-tests on untransformed per-cell mean MSD within each ligand condition, with Bonferroni correction applied across the two pairwise comparisons (EGF: adjusted P = 0.0652; TGFβ: adjusted P < 0.0001).
To test whether the inhibitory effect differed significantly between ligand conditions—rather than inferring state selectivity solely from contrasting per-ligand significance thresholds—we fit a two-way ANOVA (ligand × Hesperadin treatment) on log2-transformed per-cell mean MSD. The ligand-by-treatment interaction term was highly significant F1,2508 = 81.1, P < 2 × 10⁻¹⁶, confirming state-dependent differential sensitivity to Hesperadin. Log₂ transformation was applied exclusively for the interaction ANOVA and density-axis plotting; per-ligand Welch's t-tests were evaluated on untransformed values.
BiTE flow-cytometry and CyTOF analyses
CyTOF target-cell–PBMC coculture and analysis. A549 WT, MON1B-knockout and SH3KBP1-knockout target cells were cultured with TGFβ and cocultured with PBMCs from one donor; T-cell receptor signaling was supplied with anti-CD3/CD28, brefeldin A and monensin were added after 1–2 hours, and cocultures were incubated overnight before intracellular staining with a 50-marker panel and acquisition on a CyTOF XT (Standard BioTools). Processed FCS files underwent bead, Gaussian-parameter, event-length, viability and dual-DNA cleanup. Marker intensities were transformed using arcsinh with cofactor 5. The displayed profiles and quadrant percentages summarized all cleaned events within each sample. Fixed positivity thresholds were TNFα = 1.00, IFNγ = 1.63, MIP-1β = 1.61, Granzyme B = 1.44 and CD107a = 1.02. Cytokine-positive events were defined as positive for at least one of TNFα, IFNγ or MIP-1β; dual-positive degranulation-marker events were positive for both Granzyme B and CD107a. MIP-1β and Perforin intensities were displayed as continuous hexbin color values. Percentages were calculated separately within each FCS sample.
Activation-density plots. Retained unmixed FCS files were transformed using arcsinh with cofactor 1,000. Shared Otsu thresholds were calculated from pooled events across the displayed conditions for CD45 and CD4, and the resulting CD45⁺CD4⁺ events were used for the activation-density plots. Shared Otsu thresholds for CD69 and CD25 defined the dual-positive quadrant. Dual-positive fractions were calculated separately for each replicate well and summarized as mean ± SEM across three wells per condition from one donor.
Marker-level and multivariate summaries. Single-cell gating of flow cytometry data was performed using FlowJo (v10.8) to isolate viable CD45⁺CD4⁺ and CD45⁺CD8⁺ T-cell subsets. For each compartment, expression levels of activation, metabolic, and checkpoint markers (CD69, CD25, CD62L, CD71, CD98, and PD-1) were quantified by calculating the geometric mean fluorescence intensity (gMFI) across internal replicates. Bivariate gating on CD69 and CD62L partitioned events into four activation quadrants (CD69⁺CD62L⁻, CD69⁺CD62L⁺, CD69⁻CD62L⁺, CD69⁻CD62L⁻), and an integrated T-cell Activation Index was defined as:
Activation Index = 100 × (gMFI(CD69)) / (gMFI(CD62L))
To quantify baseline-adjusted responses, within-genotype EMT-induced changes were calculated as the ratio of gMFI in TGF-β 1–conditioned targets relative to untreated parental targets (gMFI_(TGFβ) / gMFI_(Parental)). Multivariate profiles were evaluated by principal-component analysis using stats::prcomp in R. The input matrix comprised twelve gMFI features: PD-1, CD98, CD25, CD69, CD62L and CD71 in each of the CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ compartments. Profiles with complete measurements were retained, and each feature was centered and scaled to unit variance. Principal components and feature loadings were obtained by singular value decomposition of the centered and scaled data matrix.
Bispecific antibody co-culture activation assay. The bispecific antibody targets EphA2 on tumor cells and CD3 on T cells, synthesized in-house in a single-chain variable fragment (scFv) tandem dimer format (LBD-CRD-FD fusion linker). Target A549 cells (WT, MON1B-KO, or SH3KBP1-KO) were stimulated with 10 ng/mL recombinant human TGFβ for 5 days to induce EMT, and washed three times with PBS to remove residual ligand. Effector PBMCs or purified T cells from another healthy donor (separate from CyTOF experiments) were added at an Effector:Target ratio of 1:1. Three internal wells were plated per condition, stimulated with 200 ng/mL bispecific antibody cocktail, and co-cultured for 18 hours at 37°C. Cells were stained for CD4, CD8, CD25, CD69, CD62L, and PD-1, and analyzed on a Cytek Aurora spectral flow cytometer with spectral unmixing.
QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS
Statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.3) and Python (version 3.12.5) unless otherwise specified. The biological unit, observational unit and replication structure are specified for each analysis. Data are reported as mean ± SD, mean ± SE, or effect estimate with confidence interval as specified in the figure legends. For the outcome-annotated Barrett's cohort, the patient core is both the biological and the inferential unit (18 cores from 18 distinct patients), and no patient contributes more than one core; tiles, puncta and single cells from the co-registered sections are within-specimen observational units and are reported as exploratory.
Clinical ordered-stage trends were assessed with Jonckheere–Terpstra tests. Pairwise unpaired comparisons used Wilcoxon rank-sum tests and matched NAT–lesion comparisons used paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Stage means for hybrid-centric metrics used 2,000 bootstrap resamples. Clinical, assembly and enrichment analyses used BH false-discovery-rate correction where specified. Targeted imaging used within-image Local Moran and label-permutation tests with BH correction across image-level tests; cell-level effect sizes were descriptive. Statistical significance was assessed using 10,000 label permutations. Exact statistical tests and sample sizes (n) are reported in the corresponding Methods sections and figure legends. For prespecified confirmatory analyses, P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction, with adjusted P < 0.05 considered statistically significant
